<h1>THE NOTTINGHAM AGREEMENT: ANALYSIS AND THEORIES</h1>

This section provides:
<ul>
<li>an analysis of the people, interests, options and criteria that apply to the negotiation case study</li>
<li>the key theories that students on the course have encountered that inform interactions within the roleplay.</li> 
</ul>

<h2>1.	Analysis of People, Interests, options and criteria for the negotiation</h2>
<h3>1.1 People</h3>
Consultancy team: a senior consultant, a project manager and a technical expert who provide advice and solutions. 
Client representatives: Decision-makers at the Nottingham Schools, such as the IT manager, the finance officer and department heads. (but note that there is only one client representative taking part in the actual negotiation, and may not have authority to agree certain terms).

<h3>1.2 Interests</h3>
Consultancy interests: Deliver high-quality service and maintain reputation, achieve fair compensation and build long-term client relationships 
Client interests: Obtain value for money and effective IT solutions, ensure minimal disruption and timely delivery and protect organisational priorities and compliance requirements 

<h3>1.3 Options</h3>
<p>These are potential solutions or approaches that satisfy both parties’ interests:</p>
<ul>
<li>Phased implementation plan (e.g., updated software first, then user manuals and training later)</li>
<li>Reduced scope (e.g. delivering highest priority software packages first)</li>
<li>Flexible payment plans (e.g. some payment in current financial cycle, and rest paid in next cycle)</li>
<li>Pilot programme that is scaled-up (e.g., when more funding becomes available)</li>
<li>Commitment to future contracts</li>
<li>Etc.</li>

</ul>

<h3>1.4 Criteria</h3>
These are the objective standards or benchmarks used to evaluate options fairly: 
<ul>
<li>Market rates for IT consultancy services </li>
<li>Industry best practices for IT installation and support</li>
<li>Client budget constraints and regulatory requirements</li>
<li>greed timelines and service level agreements (SLAs) 
</li>
</ul>


<h2>2.	Negotiation theories you can apply in this negotiation</h2>

<h3>2.1 Definition</h3>
Negotiation is a process in which two or more parties with differing interests communicate and explore options to reach an agreement that satisfies their needs. The goal is not to “win” over the other side but to find a solution that is acceptable to all parties.  
A good negotiator is someone who:
<ul>
<li>Separates people from the problem by addressing issues without damaging relationships. </li>
<li>Focuses on interests, not positions by looking beyond rigid demands to understand underlying needs and concerns.</li>
<li>Generates options for mutual gain by creatively exploring solutions that satisfy both parties’ interests. </li>
<li>Insists on objective criteria by using fair standards or independent benchmarks rather than pressure or subjective opinions. </li>
</ul>
A good negotiator seeks a win-win outcome, maintains good relationships, and bases agreements on reason and fairness rather than leverage or tactics. We call this “principled negotiation.” 

<h3>2.2 Distributive negotiation</h3>
This is often called a “zero-sum” or win-lose negotiation–when the resources being negotiated are fixed and limited. In this type of negotiation, the gain of one party directly corresponds to the loss of the other. The primary focus is on claiming value, meaning each party tries to maximise their share of the limited resources. 
<ul>
<li>Resources are limited, so one party’s gain is another party’s loss.</li>
<li>The goal of each party is often in direct conflict. </li>
<li>Strategies tend to be competitive: calm, crafty and self-focused.</li>
 <li>Typically involves aggressive bargaining, aiming to secure the largest possible share. </li>
 <li>Outcomes are usually short-term with little concern for long-term relationships. </li>
</ul>

<h3>2.3 Integrative negotiation</h3>
This is also called “win-win” negotiation, focuses on creating value rather than merely claiming it. Parties explore multiple issues and look for ways to expand the resources or benefits available, aiming for mutual gain. The emphasis is on collaboration and problem-solving to satisfy the interests of all parties.
<ul>
<li>Focuses on value creation rather than just division. </li>
 <li>Multiple issues are considered, allowing flexibility in trade-offs. </li>
 <li>Encourages generating creative options to “expand the pie”.</li>
 <li>Outcomes aim for mutual gain and long-term relationships.</li>
 <li>Promotes trust, cooperation and collaborative problem-solving. </li>
</ul>

<h3>2.4 Principled negotiation</h3>
Principled negotiation is an approach designed to help people reach wise agreements efficiently and amicably. Instead of arguing over positions (“I want X”), it focuses on understanding deeper interests and finding solutions that benefit both parties. The four principles are people, interests, options and criteria.  

<h4>2.4.1 People – Separate the people from the problem</h4>
Human relationships can easily become entangled with the issue being negotiated. Misunderstandings, emotions or communication breakdowns can turn a simple disagreement into a personal conflict. Therefore, we should: 
<ul>
<li>Treat the relationship and the problem as two separate things. </li>
 <li>Build trust through respect, empath and clear communication. </li>
 <li>Manage emotions, both your own and theirs, by acknowledging feelings and allowing expression.</li>
 <li>Avoid blaming by focusing on solving the issue collaboratively. </li>
 <li>Listen actively and make sure both parties feel heard. </li>
</ul>
 
<h4>2.4.2 Interests – Focus on interests, not positions</h4>
Interest: “We have a strict budget and must remain within this year’s funding restrictions.” 
It’s important to explore beneath the surface to understand the underlying motivations of each party. Focusing on interests enables creative solutions that satisfy both sides. Therefore, we should: 
<ul>
<li>Ask questions like: “Why is that important to you?” </li>
<li>Identify your own interests clearly.</li>
<li>Recognise that interests often overlap, even when positions clash. </li>
<li>Look for common ground where shared goals often exist even in conflict. </li>
<li>	Address both sides’ key concerns. </li>

<h4>2.4.3 Options – Invent options for mutual gain</h4>
Once interests are understood, negotiators can generate a range of possible solutions before deciding what to do by:  
<ul>
<li>Brainstorming multiple options without commitment. </li>
<li>Looking for creative trade-offs where each side gains on issues they value most. </li>
<li>Avoiding thinking there is only one solution (a common negotiation trap). </li>
<li>Separating option generation (creative thinking) from decision-making. </li> 
</ul>
More options increase the chance of finding a solution that meets both sides’ interests. 
 
<h4>2.4.4 Criteria – Insist on using objective criteria</h4>
When interests conflict and emotions are high, negotiations can stall. Instead of arguing over what each side prefers, both should agree to use independent standards to guide the outcome. Objective criteria help parties make fair decisions without relying on pressure or power. Objective criteria may include: 
<ul>
<li>Market value </li>
<li>Industry standards</li>
<li>Legal requirements</li>
<li>Expert opinions </li>
<li>Benchmark prices</li>
<li>Safety or quality standards </li>
<li>Precedents</li> 
</ul> 
This leads to agreements that feel fair, reduces resentment and strengthens long-term relationships. 

<h3>2.5 Obstacles obstructing the inventing of options</h3>

<h4>2.5.1 Premature judgment</h4>
This happens when a consultant “jumps to conclusions” before gathering enough evidence. This often leads to misdiagnosis, missed opportunities and biased decisions. 
To avoid this, the consultant can pause solution framing and return to structured problem diagnosis by: 
<ul>
<li>Clarifying the client’s objectives (profitability, growth or resilience)</li>
<li>Asking exploratory questions across revenue, cost and capability drivers</li>
<li>Testing multiple hypotheses before committing to a solution path</li>
</ul>

<h4>2.5.2 “Searching for a single answer”</h4>
<p>This is a thinking trap where someone assumes that there is only one correct solution to a problem, rather than recognising that complex issues often have multiple viable options.  
To avoid this, the consultant can deliberatrly generate and test multiple viable options through:</p>
<ul>
<li>Developing several operating model scenarios</li>
<li>Evaluating trade-offs rather than seeking a “perfect” solution</li>
<li>Aligning options to the client’s strategy, culture and constraints</li>
</ul>

<h4>2.5.3 The “fixed pie assumption”</h4>
<p>This is a thinking trap where someone believes that resources, value or benefits are limited, so that if one party gains, another must lose. 
To avoid this, the consultant can shift to value-based thinking through:</p>
<ul>
<li>Exploring alternative engagement structures (scope phasing, outcome-based fees)</li>
<li>Clarifying what value means for both parties</li>
<li>Co-designing solutions that expand total value rather than divide it</li>
</ul>


<h4>2.5.4 Thinking that ‘solving their problem’ is their problem</h4>
This is a thinking trap where someone assumes that others’ challenges are not their concern, so they avoid helping or addressing issues that aren’t directly their responsibility. 
To avoid this, the consulting can adopt a partnership mindset by: 
<ul>
<li>Surfacing misalignment diplomatically</li>
<li>Facilitating stakeholder discussions and decision forums </li>
<li>Supporting not just analysis, but adoption and execution</li>
</ul>

<h3>2.6 Preparing for negotiation</h3>

<h4>2.6.1 Negotiation Goals</h4>
These are the outcomes the consultant aims to achieve through the negotiation. They may include securing the project, agreeing on fees and scope, establishing timelines, managing risk and building a long-term client relationship. Clear goals help guide priorities and trade-offs during the negotiation. 
Example: Consider a salary negotiation between a candidate and a company, where the candidate expects a salary of £35,000 per year and the company has a budget cap of £40,000. The candidate’s goal is to secure a salary as close as possible to £40,000 while remaining within the acceptable range above their reservation value of £35,000, and to reach an agreement that reflects their skills and market value while meeting the company’s budget constraints. 

<h4>2.6.2 Main issues</h4>
Main issues are the specific points that need to be agreed upon. In consulting, these often include project scope, deliverables, fees, payment terms, timelines, resource allocation, confidentiality, intellectual property and risk-sharing arrangements. 
Example: For the salary negotiation case above, the main issues in the negotiation are: 
<ul>
<li>Salary level – agreeing on a final salary within the range of £35,000 to £40,000.</li>
<li>Value of the role – how the candidate’s skills, experience and qualifications justify the salary requested.</li>
<li>Budget constraints – the company’s need to stay within its £40,000 budget cap.</li>
<li>Overall compensation package – potential trade-offs such as bonuses, benefits, training opportunities or future salary review timelines. </li>
</ul>

<h4>2.6.3 Reservation Value (RV)</h4>
The reservation value is the minimum acceptable outcome the consultant is willing to accept before walking away from the negotiation. It’s a parties walk away point, representing the point at which continuing the negotiation is no longer beneficial compared to alternatives. 
Example: From the example above, the candidate’s reservation value or walk away point is £35,000, while the company’s reservation value or walk away point is £40,000. 

<h4>2.6.4 Zone of Possible Agreement (ZOPA)</h4>
ZOPA is the range within which both parties’ reservation values overlap, making agreement possible. Identifying the ZOPA helps consultants understand where mutually acceptable solutions may exist and avoid unrealistic positions. 
Example: Following the salary negotiation example above, the candidate’s reservation value is £35,000, while the company’s budget cap is £40,000. Therefore, the ZOPA lies between £35,000 and £40,000, leaving room for negotiation. 

<h4>2.6.5 Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement (BATNA)</h4>
BATNA is the best course of action available if the negotiation does not result in an agreement. In consulting, this might include pursuing another client, reallocating resources to different projects or delaying engagement. A strong BATNA increases bargaining power. 
Example: Following the salary negotiation example above, the candidate’s BATNA is accepting an alternative job offer, staying in their current role or continuing the job search. The company’s BATNA includes hiring another suitable candidate within budget or reopening the recruitment process. 

<h4>2.6.6 Question Assumptions</h4>
This involves critically examining assumptions about the client’s needs, constraints, priorities and alternatives. Challenging assumptions can uncover hidden interests, reveal new options and prevent misunderstandings that could hinder agreement. 
Example: Following the example above, here are examples of question assumptions for both parties: 
<ul>
<li>Whether £35,000 truly reflects the candidate’s market value</li>
<li>Whether the £40,000 budget is fully fixed or flexible </li>
<li>Whether non-monetary benefits could compensate for a lower salary </li>
<li>Whether there are alternative ways to structure the offer to meet both parties’ interests</li> 
</ul>

<h4>2.6.7 Negotiation strategy</h4>
The negotiation strategy outlines how the consultant plans to achieve their goals. It includes decisions on negotiation style (e.g. distributive or integrative), sequencing of issues, use of concessions, communication approach and how to manage risks and relationships throughout the engagement. 

<h3>2.7 Negotiation power</h3>

<h4>2.7.1 Power of Authority</h4>
Effective negotiation requires engagement with individuals who possess the legitimate authority to make binding decisions. For example, confirming whether a client representative can approve a contract ensures that the discussion yields actionable outcomes, therefore avoiding inefficient use of resources. 

<h4>2.7.2 Power of Knowledge of the Needs of the Other Party</h4>
A successful negotiator demonstrates a nuanced understanding of the counterpart’s priorities and constraints. For instance, recognising a school’s need for a modernised timetable system within strict budgetary limits enables the consultant to propose tailored solutions that align with the client’s objectives. 

<h4>2.7.3 Power of Knowledge and Expertise</h4>
Expertise establishes credibility and persuasive influence in negotiations. Demonstrating prior experience in implementing education-specific IT solutions signals competence and reliability, thereby increasing the client’s confidence in the proposed intervention. 

<h4>2.7.4 Power of Preparation</h4>
Comprehensive preparation underpins effective negotiation. Anticipating potential objections, developing alternative solutions and accurately estimating costs allows the consultant to engage confidently and strategically, reducing the likelihood of reactive or suboptimal decisions. 

<h4>2.7.5 Power of Identification</h4>
Building rapport through shared professional experiences or values fosters trust and facilitates constructive dialogue. Establishing common ground with the client can enhance relational dynamics, promoting cooperative problem-solving and mutually beneficial outcomes. 

<h4>2.7.6 Power of Investment</h4>
The consultant’s prior investment of time, effort and resources functions as a source of leverage. Engagements involving significant preparatory work signal commitment and expertise, increasing the perceived cost to the client of failing to reach an agreement. 

<h4>2.7.7 Power of Scarcity</h4>
Limited availability of resources or project timelines can create a sense of urgency. Emphasising constrained scheduling or finite capacity may incentivise the client to make timely decisions, thereby enhancing the consultant’s negotiating position. 

<h4>2.7.8 Power of Indifference</h4>
Maintaining composure and demonstrating impartiality communicates confidence and strategic detachment. Displaying measured responses to low or challenging offers signals that the consultant is not compelled to accept suboptimal terms, potentially eliciting more favourable concessions. 

<h4>2.7.9 Power of Courage</h4>
The consultant’s willingness to assert the value of their proposal with confidence reinforces credibility. Firmly advocating for an £85,000 project cost, for example, demonstrates conviction in the quality and strategic importance of the services offered. 

<h4>2.7.10 Power of Personality</h4>
Interpersonal attributes such as empathy, professionalism and approachability enhance relational influence. Positive relational engagement can facilitate openness, trust and collaborative problem-solving, therefore improving negotiation outcomes. 
 
<h4>2.7.11 Power of Body Language</h4>
Non-verbal communication constitutes a significant component of persuasive influence. Open posture, direct eye contact and controlled gestures convey confidence and credibility, whereas incongruent or defensive body language may undermine persuasive authority. 

<h4>2.7.12 Power of Reciprocity</h4>
Strategic concessions or value-added offers can generate reciprocal behaviour from the client. Providing additional documentation or training without immediate compensation may induce the client to accommodate budgetary adjustments or other concessions in return. 

<h4>2.7.13 Power of Justification</h4>
Providing rational explanations for proposals strengthens their legitimacy. Articulating that the £85,000 project cost reflects the complexity of system customisation and staff training helps the client understand and accept the pricing rationale. 

<h4>2.7.14 Power of Social Proof</h4>
Referencing prior engagements or outcomes achieved for similar clients' functions as a form of social validation. Demonstrating successful adoption of comparable solutions by other institutions reduces perceived risk and enhances credibility. 

<h4>2.7.15 Power of Logic</h4>
A structured, evidence-based argument enhances persuasive efficacy. Presenting a stepwise project plan, with defined timelines, expected outcomes and measurable benefits, appeals to rational decision-making and supports objective evaluation of the proposal. 

<h3>2.8 Negotiation strategy</h3>

<h4>2.8.1 Negotiation Tactics in Consulting Engagements</h4>
In consulting engagements, negotiation tactics are the deliberate actions consultants use to influence the negotiation process, protect professional value and shape mutually acceptable agreements. The following are negotiation tactics the consultant could employ during the negotiation to enhance the success of the outcomes. 

<h5>Anchoring in Consulting Negotiations</h5>
Consultants often use anchoring to establish a reference point for value. An initial proposal fee, project duration or scope definition sets expectations and frames subsequent discussions. Because consulting outputs are difficult to price objectively, early anchors significantly influence how clients perceive fairness and affordability. 

<h5>Framing Around Value Rather Than Cost</h5>
Consulting negotiations benefit from framing the discussion in terms of outcomes, impact and risk reduction instead of price. Consultants emphasise strategic benefits, efficiency gains or capability development to counteract client focus on budget constraints. 

<h5>Diagnostic Questioning</h5>
Consultants rely heavily on questioning tactics to uncover client needs, constraints and priorities. Open-ended, probing questions help reveal whether positions are fixed or negotiable and expose underlying interests such as risk aversion, timelines or political constraints. 

<h5>Issue Packaging and Trade-Offs</h5>
Rather than negotiating single issues in isolation, consultants package multiple elements, including scope, timeline, staffing, deliverables and support, into integrated proposals. This enables trade-offs that protect core value while offering flexibility. 

<h5>Conditionality and Reciprocity</h5>
In consulting negotiations, concessions should always be conditional. Consultants use “if–then” statements to ensure movement is reciprocal and aligned with professional boundaries. 

<h5>Use of Objective Standards and Justification</h5>
Consultants frequently justify positions using external benchmarks, industry standards, day rates or comparable engagements. This shifts negotiations from subjective bargaining to legitimacy-based discussion. 

<h5>Managing Scope Creep Through Tactical Clarity</h5>
A critical consulting tactic involves clearly defining scope boundaries and change mechanisms. Consultants tactically reinforce what is included, excluded and how changes will be managed contractually. 

<h5>Strategic Use of Indifference</h5>
Consultants may signal, subtly and professionally, that they are prepared to walk away if terms undermine quality or sustainability. This tactic relies on confidence in one’s BATNA rather than confrontation. 

<h5>Sequencing and Phasing Tactics</h5>
Consultants often propose phased engagements to align client budgets with consultant fees. This tactic allows agreement without reducing total project value. 

<h5>Relationship-Preserving Tactics</h5>
Because consulting engagements often lead to repeat work, tactics are deployed with relational sensitivity. Consultants balance firmness with empathy, ensuring the negotiation does not damage trust. 
 
<h4>2.8.2 Concessions in Consulting Engagements</h4>
Concessions are intentional compromises made during a negotiation to move closer to agreement. In consulting engagements, concessions are strategic adjustments made to facilitate agreement while protecting professional value and delivery quality. These concessions typically fall into three categories: substantive, process and symbolic. 

<h5>2.8.2.1 Substantive Concessions</h5>
Substantive concessions directly affect the economic or operational core of the consulting engagement, such as price, scope or timelines. These concessions have the greatest impact on project viability and therefore require careful control.
<ul>
<li>Price concessions involve reductions in fees and should be approached with caution, as they directly affect profitability and may set undesirable precedents.</li>
<li>Scope concessions adjust what is delivered, such as removing non-essential features or reducing the depth of customisation.</li>
<li>Timeline concessions alter delivery schedules, for example extending deadlines to reduce resource intensity.</li>
</ul>
In consulting, substantive concessions should be rare, incremental, and closely aligned with the consultant’s reservation value. 
 
<h5>2.8.2.2 Process Concessions</h5>
Process concessions relate to how the consulting work is delivered or managed, rather than what is delivered. These concessions often have lower cost implications and are therefore more flexible. 
<ul>
<li>Payment term concessions include staged payments or deferred invoicing to accommodate client cash-flow constraints. </li>
<li>Phasing concessions involve breaking the engagement into stages, allowing the client to spread costs over time. </li>
<li>Scheduling concessions may include flexible start dates or adjusted meeting frequency. </li> 
</ul>
Process concessions are particularly effective in consulting as they preserve overall value while addressing client constraints. 
 
<h5>2.8.2.3 Symbolic Concessions</h5>
Symbolic concessions are low-cost, high-perceived-value adjustments that primarily serve relational and trust-building purposes.
<ul>
<li>Extra documentation enhances knowledge transfer and client capability.</li>
<li>Additional training sessions support adoption and long-term impact. </li>
<li>Extended support or check-ins signal commitment beyond contractual minimums. </li> 
</ul>
Although symbolic concessions have minimal economic cost, they should still be framed as intentional and conditional to avoid being taken for granted. 
 
<h4>2.8.3 Risk Management in Consulting Negotiations</h4>
Risk management in consulting negotiations involves the systematic identification, assessment and mitigation of risks that may undermine commercial viability, delivery quality or the long-term client relationship. The following are key risk aspects that consultants should consider when developing a negotiation strategy for a consulting engagement. 

<h5>2.8.3.1 Pricing and Profitability Risk</h5>
The consultant could potentially accept fees below the sustainable level due to client budget pressure, leading to financial loss or compromised delivery quality. This risk can be mitigated through:
<ul>
<li>Defining and adhering to a clear reservation value (RV).</li>
<li>Avoiding early or unilateral price concessions.</li>
<li>Using scope, phasing or process concessions instead of fee reductions.</li>
<li>Justifying pricing using objective standards and cost breakdowns.</li> 
</ul>
 
<h5>2.8.3.2 Scope Creep Risk</h5>
Sometimes, there could be an uncontrolled expansion of project scope during or after negotiation increases workload without corresponding compensation. This risk can be mitigated by: 
<ul>
<li>Clearly defining inclusions, exclusions and assumptions in the proposal. </li>
<li>Using phased delivery with explicit deliverables per phase. </li>
<li>Establishing formal change-control mechanisms linked to pricing. </li>
<li>Treating scope adjustments as renegotiation triggers. </li>
</ul>

<h5>2.8.3.3 Expectation Misalignment Risk</h5>
Client expectations regarding outcomes, timelines or effort could differ from what the consultant intends to deliver. This could be avoided through: 
<ul>
<li>Reframing discussions around deliverables, not aspirations. </li>
<li>Using logic and visual project plans to clarify timelines and dependencies.</li>
<li>Summarising agreements in writing during negotiations. </li>
<li>Validating understanding through diagnostic questioning. </li>
</ul>
 
<h5>2.8.3.4 Authority and Decision-Making Risk</h5>
The consultant could possibly negotiate with a client representative who lacks decision-making authority, delaying or invalidating agreement. This could be mitigated by:
<ul>
<li>Early identification of decision-makers and budget holders. </li>
<li>Explicitly confirming approval authority. </li>
<li>Requesting joint meetings with all key stakeholders when necessary.</li> 
</ul>

<h5>2.8.3.5 Relationship and Trust Risk</h5>
Hard bargaining on price or scope could damage trust and undermine long-term collaboration. This can be mitigated though: 
<ul>
<li>Maintaining professional tone and empathy when resisting concessions.</li>
<li>Using symbolic concessions to signal goodwill.</li>
<li>Separating people from the problem (interest-based negotiation).</li>
<li>Acknowledging constraints without conceding value. </li> 
</ul>
 
<h5>2.8.3.6 Precedent and Reputation Risk</h5>
Accepting discounted terms could set a precedent that weakens future negotiations or damage brand positioning. This risk can be mitigated by: 
<ul>
<li>Clearly framing any concessions as exceptional and context-specific.</li>
<li>Avoiding public or informal commitments that bypass formal agreement.</li>
<li>Protecting standard pricing structures through justification and benchmarking. </li>
</ul>

<h5>2.8.3.7 BATNA Misjudgement Risk</h5>
Overestimating the value of the deal or underestimating alternatives could lead to weak negotiation positions.This could be mitigated by: 
<ul>
<li>Conducting realistic BATNA analysis before negotiation.</li>
<li>Periodically reassessing BATNA as negotiations evolve.</li>
<li>Signaling willingness to walk away when necessary.</li>
</ul>

<h5>2.8.3.8 Emotional and Cognitive Bias Risk</h5>
Frustration, over-commitment or desire to “win the deal” could lead to poor judgment. This can be mitigated by: 
<ul>
<li>Separating strategic decisions from emotional reactions.</li>
<li>Using preparation documents and decision thresholds.</li>
<li>Pausing negotiations when pressure escalates. </li>
<li>Involving a second consultant or partner for objectivity. </li> 
</ul>

<h5>2.8.3.9 Implementation and Delivery Risk</h5>
Negotiation outcomes could create delivery conditions that are unrealistic or unsustainable. This ca be mitigated by:
<ul>
<li>Stress-testing negotiated terms against delivery capacity. </li>
<li>Aligning timelines and staffing with operational reality. </li>
<li>Rejecting agreements that compromise professional standards. 
</li>
</ul>

<h3>2.9	The Power of Four</h3>
The Law of Four emphasises sequential negotiation, rather than trying to resolve all issues at once. It focuses on putting aside the main issues (e.g. price bargaining) and focusing on relationship building with the client. 
This approach reduces conflict by deferring contentious points until mutual understanding is built. This approach is particularly useful in consulting, where contracts often involve complex deliverables, fees and risk clauses.

<h4>2.9.1	Step 1 - Identify the main issues and put them aside</h4>
The negotiation starts with the consultant recognising all the key points of contention or negotiation for both parties. These main issues are temporarily set aside to avoid getting stuck on details early in the negotiation.
Example: The consultant recognises the main issues which are price, scope, timeline and training, but deliberately sets them aside at the start to avoid getting stuck on budget disagreements too early.

<h4>2.9.2	Step 2 - Revisiting the request for proposals, draft agreement, or other project documents, focus on underlying terms and conditions</h4>
After analysing the main issues for both parties and putting them aside temporarily, the consultant then examines the request for proposals, draft agreement or other project documents carefully. They should focus on terms and conditions (if any) within the documents, not just surface-level disagreements. This step helps clarify priorities and mutual interests of both parties.
Example: The consultant reviews the draft agreement to clarify project goals, responsibilities and success criteria, identifying that reliability and ease of use are key priorities for the school.

<h4>2.9.3	Step 3 - In the second iteration, work on disagreements</h4>
Once the main issues and terms are understood, the consultant begins addressing the points of disagreement. They should start negotiating specific conflicts with a clearer understanding of the overall agreement.
Example: With priorities clear, the consultant begins addressing disagreements by proposing options, such as reducing scope or phasing delivery to better fit the school’s budget.

<h4>2.9.4	Step 4 - Revise disagreement and arrive at the main issues</h4>
The consultant reviews progress and updates the draft to reflect resolved disagreements. Finally, they return to the main issues with a structured approach, aiming to reach a mutually acceptable outcome.
Example: The consultant updates the agreement to reflect resolved points and then returns to the fee discussion, linking price to the revised scope and delivery plan.
